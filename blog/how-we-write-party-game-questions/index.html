How we write party game questions people actually answer

We have written thousands of cards for Antics, and killed a few thousand more. Here is what we learned about the difference between a question that gets a gasp and one that gets a shrug.

Most party game questions are bad in the same way. They are written to be safe, so they end up being about nobody. "Have you ever told a lie?" is a question that cannot fail and cannot land, because everyone has, and so nobody has anything to say. The whole craft is getting away from that, and towards questions that make one specific person go very quiet.

Specific beats general, every time

The single biggest lever is specificity. Compare two lines:

Never have I ever kissed a stranger.
Never have I ever kissed someone whose name I never learned.

They mean almost the same thing. The first is a category; the second is a scene. The detail does the work: it puts a picture in everyone's head, and it gives the person it applies to nowhere to hide. Generic questions get a nod. Specific ones get a story, and the story is the point.

Write for a room of mates, not an exam

The fastest way to kill a question is to make it sound like it was written by a committee. Real groups do not talk in full, careful sentences, and a card that does feels like a form to fill in. We write the way people actually speak on a sofa: short, a bit rude, occasionally unfair. If a line would make your group laugh when you read it out, it stays. If it sounds like a personality quiz, it goes.

Aim the question at someone

A question that could be about anyone lands on no one. The best prompts have a target built in, either by naming a role ("the person on your left") or by being so specific that the room instantly knows who it is about before anyone has answered. Half the fun of a good card is the two seconds of silence while everyone looks at the same person.

Sort by heat, not at random

A deck is not a flat list. Open with warm-up questions that anyone can answer, so the shy players get in early, then let it get bolder as the room does. Lead with your best material rather than burying it, because most groups will only see the first stretch of any deck in a night. Every one of our games is sorted this way, warm-up to spicy, so the night has somewhere to build to.

Cut more than you keep

The honest part: most of what we draft dies. For every card that ships, several get written, read out loud, land flat, and get deleted. The test is always the same, and it is not "is this clever", it is "will this make a real group react". A lot of clever lines fail that test. We would rather ship fewer cards that all work than pad the deck with filler nobody remembers.
