Party games for small groups: what works with three or four people

Half the group cancelled, or it was only ever going to be the three of you. Either way, most party games are written for a living room of ten, and some of them quietly fall apart when there are only two other people to look at. Others get sharper. Here is which is which, and how to patch the ones that wobble.

The short answer

With three or four people, pick games where every card asks something of each person, not games that need a crowd to vote or hide in. Never Have I Ever, Would You Rather, Truth or Dare and What Are The Odds all get better in a small group, because every card lands on everyone (or your turn comes straight back round) and there is nowhere to hide. Imposter works from three with a few tweaks. Most Likely To and anything built on pointing is the weakest with three, and needs a house rule for ties.

None of it needs equipment. One phone gets passed round, and that is the whole kit.

Why some games wobble with three

It comes down to three kinds of card.

Cards everyone answers. Never Have I Ever is the clearest case: the card is read out, and anyone who has done it owns up. Nothing about that needs a big group. With three of you, each confession gets the whole room's attention, and the follow-up questions are longer and nosier.

Cards that take turns. Truth or Dare and What Are The Odds put one or two people on the spot per card. In a group of ten you can sit through a lot of other people's dares before your go. With three, it is back to you before you have finished your excuse from last time, which is the point.

Cards that need a vote. This is where small groups struggle. Most Likely To ends with everyone pointing on three, and the most votes takes the forfeit. With three players there are only three fingers to go round, so a one, one, one split, where everyone gets a vote and nobody has the most, is easy to land on. Would You Rather has the opposite luck: the app gives the forfeit to the minority, and with three voters there is always a minority unless you all agree.

So the rule of thumb is simple. An odd number of players suits Would You Rather, an even number can tie it, and pointing games need four or five before they find their feet.

Game by game: how each one plays with a small group

Imposter: works from three, better from four

All but one player see the same secret word, and the odd one out bluffs through a round of clues. The app needs at least three players and takes up to twelve. Three is a different game from eight: there is no crowd to blend into, so the imposter's only hope is to get the two who know the word suspecting each other. We wrote a whole guide to playing Imposter with 3 people, so we will not repeat it here.

Two things worth knowing for small groups. The app only offers a second imposter from six players, so a trio or a four never gets two. And Mischief mode, where some rounds nobody gets the secret and everyone bluffs at everyone, is at its most paranoid with three. Imposter is free in the app, or try a round on this site with the free imposter game, no download needed.

Most Likely To: the weakest with three

Everyone counts down from three, points at whoever fits the card, and the most votes takes the forfeit. With three players that can end in a three-way split. Two fixes work. Either the person holding the phone breaks any tie, or you drop the vote and argue it out: everyone gets ten seconds to make their case and the room settles it by shouting. From four it starts working, and from five or six it is one of the best games going.

Truth or Dare: better small

Add names and the app says whose go it is, then a "Done, next player" button hands it on, so with three of you the turn comes back every third card. Chicken out of a truth or a dare and you take a forfeit instead. Cards that ask about "this room" hit harder when the room is two other people:

  • Who in this room survives longest in a horror film, and who goes first?

With three players, that is not a question about the group. It is a ranking of everyone listening.

Never Have I Ever: works at any size

Someone reads it out, and whoever has done it owns up and takes the forfeit. It works with two and it works with twelve, but a small group is where it is most honest, because nobody can hide behind a crowd of people putting their hands up.

Would You Rather: a natural for three

Everyone votes on three and the minority does the forfeit. With three voters there is always a minority unless you all agree, so the game never stalls. With four, a two-two split has no minority: decide before you start whether that means nobody does it or both sides do.

What Are The Odds: made for two at a time

One player asks another "what are the odds" about the card. The other sets the odds, say 1 in 10, then you count down from three and both shout a number between 1 and 10. Same number, and it happens. Add names and the app calls who asks whom, moving on one seat each card; without names, whoever holds the phone asks the player on their left. Since only two people play each card, it is one of the few games that works perfectly with just two of you.

Antics mode: mostly fine, skip the votes

Antics mode mixes five kinds of card in one feed: cards for everyone, call-outs that name one player, new rules that last a set number of cards, quick challenges and votes. In a small group the statements, rules and challenges all land. The vote cards have the same three-way-split problem as Most Likely To, so use the same tie-break, or just tap on to the next card.

The Four Kings: long but lively

The classic circle game: draw from a 52-card deck and every rank has its own rule, from Rhyme and Categories to the King that adds a move to a group dance routine. With three, you are drawing every third card, so everyone gets plenty of goes. Rhyme and Categories come round fast, which makes them harder, in a good way. Two of the thirteen rules depend on who is there: one hits every girl and one hits every guy, so in a same-gender trio one of them is everybody and the other is nobody. It is free in the app.

Three example rounds with three players

Here is how three real cards from the app play out with three friends, who we will call Sam, Ali and Kat. All three cards are in the free version of Antics.

ROUND 1 What Are The Odds

  • You speak in an accent until your next turn. Group picks which.

The app says Sam asks Ali. Ali sets the odds at 1 in 10, feeling safe. Three, two, one: both shout four. In a big group, "group picks which" is a messy vote. With three, the group is Sam and Kat, who agree on something awful in about a second. Next card, the app moves the asking on, and it is Ali's turn to ask Kat, in the accent.

ROUND 2 Never Have I Ever

  • Had a dream about someone in this room.

Kat owns up and takes the forfeit. At a party of twelve, that is a shrug. At a table of three, there are exactly two suspects and both of them are staring at her. Allow one follow-up question each, and this card can carry the next ten minutes on its own.

ROUND 3 Would You Rather

  • Get a tattoo chosen by this group right now or Delete one app chosen by this group forever

Vote on three. Sam and Kat pick deleting the app, Ali picks the tattoo, so Ali is the minority and does the forfeit. With three players the vote can never tie, and "this group" is two people Ali now has to trust with a design. Ask Sam and Kat what tattoo they would have picked. It is never what Ali hoped.

Two of you

What Are The Odds is the pick, because every card is a duel between two people anyway. Truth or Dare works too, swapping turns card by card, and Never Have I Ever is fine if you do not mind there being only one other person to judge you. Would You Rather loses its vote, so play it as a guessing game instead: each of you guesses what the other will pick before you both answer, and a wrong guess does the forfeit. Antics mode works if you tap past the vote cards, and The Four Kings works too, with every rule landing on one of the two of you. Imposter needs three, and Most Likely To with two is just pointing at each other.

Three of you

The sweet spot for Would You Rather, since the vote cannot tie, and a very good size for Never Have I Ever, Truth or Dare and What Are The Odds. Imposter works with the changes in the three-player guide. Most Likely To needs a tie-break, so agree one before the first card.

Four of you

Nearly everything in the app works at four. The one thing to watch is a two-two split, in a Would You Rather, Most Likely To or Imposter vote, so settle the tie rule up front. Most Likely To starts to earn its place here, because with three other people to point at, the fingers start to pile up on someone.

Five or six of you

You are a small party now, and the pointing games come into their own: Most Likely To and the vote cards in Antics mode are at their best around this size. At six, the app also lets you deal Imposter with two imposters. Setting it up for a small group

Add the names. It takes ten seconds and matters more in a small group than a big one. With names in, the cards that call someone out use real names, Truth or Dare and What Are The Odds say whose turn it is, and The Four Kings says who draws next. Names are saved and shared across every game, so you only type them once. Without names, a card that would name someone says "your left neighbour" instead, which still works, but it is less fun when there are only two neighbours.

Agree the tie rule first. Before the first vote, decide what a split means. It saves an argument that is less fun than the game.

Tap past anything that does not fit. A card about the person opposite is odd when nobody is opposite. Move on, and the app keeps track of what it has shown your phone, so fresh cards come up before old ones repeat.

Nobody has to drink. Penalties in Antics are always forfeits, so the friend on lemonade plays exactly the same game.

Small group games: questions

What party games work best for a small group?

Games where every card involves everyone, or where your turn comes round quickly: Never Have I Ever, Would You Rather, Truth or Dare and What Are The Odds. Imposter works from three players with a few tweaks. Pointing games like Most Likely To are the weakest with three and need a tie-break rule.

What games can you play with three people?

Would You Rather is a natural fit, because a vote between three people always has a minority unless everyone agrees. Never Have I Ever, Truth or Dare and What Are The Odds all play well with three, and Imposter works if you deal it on a phone and keep the clues short. Most Likely To needs a house rule for three-way splits.

What games can two people play?

What Are The Odds is built for two: one person asks, the other sets the odds, then you both shout a number. Truth or Dare and Never Have I Ever work with two as well. Imposter needs at least three players.
